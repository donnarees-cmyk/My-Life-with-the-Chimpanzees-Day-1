    📖 Reading autobiographies

    Standard: ELA.6.R.2 · Genre: Nonfiction

    What is an autobiography?

    An autobiography is a true story about a person’s life, written by that same person. “Auto” means self and “bio” means life. The author is telling about their own life.

    📖 AutobiographyWritten by the person it is about.
    Uses I, me, my, we.
    “I was born in a small town.”
    📚 BiographyWritten by someone else about a person.
    Uses he, she, they.
    “She was born in a small town.”

    Look for these clues in an autobiography:

    • 🗣️ The author tells it in the first person (“I”).
    • 📅 It is about real events from the author’s life.
    • 💭 You read the author’s thoughts and feelings, not just what happened.
    • 🔙 The author often looks back and shares what they learned.

    🧭 Author’s purpose, message, and tone

    Standards: ELA.6.R.2 and ELA.6.R.3.1

    Three big ideas

    🎯 PurposeWHY the author wrote it. Usually to inform (teach), persuade (convince), or entertain. Autobiographies often inform and inspire.
    💌 MessageThe big lesson or idea the author wants you to take away about life.
    🎭 ToneThe author’s attitude or feeling about the topic. Think of how a voice sounds when it is happy, serious, or worried.

    Tone words:

    joyfulcuriousdeterminedproudhopefulworriedgloomyhumorousseriousrespectful

    🕵️ How to find tone: Look at the author’s word choice, the details they pick, and what they choose to emphasize. Tone is a feeling, so find the clue words that make you feel it.

    Read the passage and investigate

    Practice passage written for this tutorial (not from the selection) When I was nine, I trailed a family of ducks around our pond every afternoon, notebook in hand. My mother worried I would fall in. My teacher said ducks were not a real subject. But I kept writing down what I saw: which duckling swam last, who quacked first, who ignored the rain. By spring, my notebook was thicker than my math book. I did not know it then, but those muddy afternoons were my first science lessons. Years later, whenever people ask how I became a scientist, I tell them the answer is simple. I stayed curious, even when nobody believed me.

    🧩 Sections and text features

    Standards: ELA.6.R.2 and ELA.6.R.2.1

    Parts that help you understand

    Authors build a text out of sections and features. Each one has a job.

    • 🏷️ Headings tell you what a section is mostly about.
    • 🖼️ Captions explain a picture and add details.
    • 📦 Sidebars give extra facts that connect to the topic.
    • 🔢 Order of sections shows how the ideas or events are organized. In an autobiography, sections often go in the order things happened (time order).

    Ask yourself: “What does this part add to the meaning of the whole text?”

    🌈 Figurative language

    Standard: ELA.6.R.3.1

    Words that mean more than they say

    Figurative language uses words in a creative way, not exactly what they literally mean. It helps create a tone and adds meaning.

    SimileCompares with like or as.
    “Fast as lightning.”
    MetaphorSays one thing is another.
    “The classroom was a zoo.”
    PersonificationGives human actions to things that are not human.
    “The sun smiled down.”
    HyperboleA huge exaggeration.
    “I’ve told you a million times.”

    ✨ Two questions to ask: 1) What does it literally say? 2) What feeling or idea does it add?

    🔍 Establish a purpose for reading

    Standards: ELA.6.R.2.1 and ELA.K12.EE.2.1

    Why am I reading this?

    Good readers decide why they are reading before they start. Your purpose keeps your brain focused and helps you remember what matters.

    1. 📌 Look at the title and the genre. “My Life with the Chimpanzees” is nonfiction, and it is an autobiography.
    2. 🧠 Think about what you already know.
    3. ❓ Ask questions.
    4. ✍️ Write your purpose. Try: “I am reading to find out…”
